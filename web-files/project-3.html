Campaign concept & educational website

Australian Cancer Council: Skin Cancer Education Website

A digital platform for the iconic “Slip, Slop, Slap!” campaign, using archived posters to teach sun safety to children, parents and teachers.

Project type
Campaign concept and educational website
My role
UX/UI and web designer
Timeline
12 weeks (March 2025)
Tools
Figma, Webflow, Google Suite, Notion

Summary: The “Slip, Slop, Slap!” campaign never had a dedicated digital home, and health statistics can feel intimidating. I designed and built a story-led website in Figma and Webflow, using historical posters to teach sun safety. Feedback shaped the navigation, mobile layout and donation form, and the project earned a high distinction.

The problem

The campaign’s best-known materials date back to the 1980s, and sun safety facts are hard to make approachable.

  • No digital presence: the original campaign materials, mainly TV ads and printed illustrations, had no modern digital home.
  • An intimidating topic: statistics like UVR causing up to 95% of melanomas can overwhelm people. The facts had to become digestible without losing the health message.
  • Scope and mobile limits: ambitious multi-page layouts were hard to make responsive, especially horizontal historical timelines and galleries on mobile.

The goal

  • Digitalize and commemorate: revisit the 1981 campaign using archival posters from the Powerhouse Collection, to motivate today’s generation to adopt sun-safe habits.
  • Educate through storytelling: teach sun safety by telling the story behind the posters, as a tool for teachers, parents and children.
  • Inclusive and approachable design: simplify complex health ideas with lighthearted cartoon styles that work for all ages.

Insights and decisions

Visuals decide whether content feels accessible

Finding: intimidating health facts make users drop off, while vibrant, character-driven campaigns lower the barrier to engage.
Decision: a simple interface with lots of whitespace so the Powerhouse Collection posters stand out. After early feedback that the homepage lacked context, I added a showcase section at the top to ground visitors in the campaign’s history.

Fewer pages make a better flow

Finding: separate pages for closely related topics, like UV radiation and health risks, fragmented the reading experience and added needless navigation steps.
Decision: I merged related topics into single pages, such as UV radiation and health risks inside the main Sun Safety Guide. I also added small transitional sections between pages that nudge people to the next relevant topic.

The solution

  • Tabbed safety guide: the Guide and Safety Tips page was too long and caused scroll fatigue, especially on mobile. I split the dense content into interactive tabs, so people can open one topic at a time without losing their place.
  • Accessible donation form: the first version had misaligned labels, missing placeholders and a clunky dropdown for amounts. I added clear labels and placeholders, separated personal info from payment info, combined first and last name into one “Name” field, and swapped the dropdown for a number input so people choose their own amount.

The results

HDoverall high distinction grade with an assignment score of 98%
85Lighthouse score for desktop and mobile accessibility
87%positive feedback from peers during in-class testing sessions

A desktop Lighthouse audit of the live Webflow site flagged some minor accessibility issues. Peers gave positive feedback on the clear narrative structure and user experience. The final Webflow prototype connected historical print media with modern web standards.

What I learned

  • Scope management is critical in UX. My early ambition led to compromises, like using free assets instead of custom illustrations.
  • Scaling back let me focus on the UX details: tabbed content, a consolidated information architecture and form usability. That made the product stronger and more user-centred.
  • Reflecting on my 85 accessibility score, Lighthouse flagged three issues I would fix next: contrast ratios, missing landmarks and missing language attributes.
