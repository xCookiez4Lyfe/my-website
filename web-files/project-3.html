Cancer Council Australia: Sun Safety Campaign

A digital platform for the iconic “Slip, Slop, Slap!” campaign, using archived posters to teach sun safety to kids, parents and teachers.

Project type
Campaign concept
My role
Web designer
Timeline
12 weeks
Tools
Figma, Webflow, Miro

Summary: The “Slip, Slop, Slap!” campaign never had a dedicated digital home. I designed and built a story-led website in Figma and Webflow, using historical posters to educate people about sun safety. The project earned a high distinction grade.

The problem

  • The campaign’s best-known materials date back to the 1980s, which were mostly TV ads and printed illustrations, and had no modern digital presence.
  • Statistics about skin cancer can be intimidating and overwhelming for people. The facts had to become digestible without losing the purpose of spreading awareness.

The goal

  • Digitalise and commemorate the 1981 campaign using archival posters from the Powerhouse Collection, to motivate younger generations to adopt sun-safe habits.
  • Simplify important safety tips with a fun and lighthearted tone that is inclusive and approachable for all ages.

Insights & decisions

Visuals decide the accessibility of content

Finding: Intimidating health facts could cause user drop off, while vibrant, character-driven campaigns lower the barrier to engage.
Decision: A clean interface with lots of whitespace for visuals and posters stand out. Safety tips and statistics are presented using simple language.

Fewer pages make a better flow

Finding: Separate pages for closely related topics, fragmented the reading experience and increased navigation steps.
Decision: I merged related topics into single pages, such as UV radiation and health risks inside the main Sun Safety Guide.

Key features

  • Tabbed section: The Sun Safety Guide page was too long and caused scroll fatigue, especially on mobile. I separated content into different tabs, so readers can focus on one topic at a time.
  • Accessible donation form: I added clear labels and placeholders, separated personal info from payment info, and swapped the dropdown for a number input so people choose their own amount.

The results

5historical artifacts featured on website
100%responsive breakpoint coverage
6 → 5pages reduced to improve navigation flow

The final version of the responsive Webflow site features 5 historical campaign posters that were extracted from the Powerhouse Collection. Merging related topics helped reduce the website page count and scroll fatigue, and the layout works across mobile breakpoints without clipping.

What I learned

  • Scope management is critical in UX. My early ambition led to compromises, like using free assets from Flaticon instead of custom illustrations.
  • Scaling back let me focus on the UX details: tabbed content, strong information architecture and form usability. That made the product stronger and more user-centred.
